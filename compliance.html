Why This Matters

Regulation moved the burden
of proof onto the buyer.

A decade ago, a European company could buy from a supplier and take their word for it. That era has ended.

Under the EU Batteries Regulation, the EU Deforestation Regulation and, from July 2029 for the largest companies, the Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive, the company placing goods on the European market carries the legal obligation to prove those goods are traceable, legally produced and free of human rights abuse. Fail, and you lose market access.

That proof cannot be assembled from a desk in Europe. It has to be gathered at source, by people who can visit the sites, speak the languages and be trusted by the communities. That is exactly where we work.

Where the Pressure Is Highest

Cobalt is where this regulation
bites first.

Around 70% of the world's cobalt originates in the Democratic Republic of Congo. It sits in every electric vehicle, phone and laptop battery sold in Europe.

Under the EU Batteries Regulation, any company placing batteries on the European market must prove that cobalt is responsibly sourced, traceable, and free of human rights abuse. The obligation falls on the importer. But the evidence sits with the producer, thousands of miles away, in a language they do not speak.

That gap is the whole problem. Closing it is the whole service.

The local rules matter. Since 16 October 2025, cobalt leaves the DRC under an export quota system run by ARECOMS, with a yearly cap and monthly allocations. Hand-dug cobalt can only be bought and sold through the state company EGC. A cooperative that sells outside that route is not a compliant source, however good its site conditions. We check where a cooperative sits in that system before we check anything else.

We support both sides of it: Congolese exporters who must produce the evidence, and European buyers who must hold it. ICONGRP has an established operation in Kinshasa, a small field team in Kolwezi, Lualaba province, where most monitored cobalt sites are, and a UK compliance team that knows what regulators and notified bodies will accept.

Beyond cobalt

The same model applies wherever the burden of proof outruns the available evidence. The EU Deforestation Regulation places identical obligations on timber, cocoa, coffee, rubber, soy, palm oil and cattle. The CSDDD extends the principle across the supply chains of the largest companies. As our clients' obligations widen, so does our practice.

Our Method

What a site verification
involves.

01

Desk review

Before anyone travels we check the cooperative's registration, its position in the EGC and ARECOMS system, its export records and any previous audit findings.

02

Site visits

Two visits, the second unannounced. We photograph conditions, record GPS, count and observe the workforce, check safety provision and look at how material is weighed, bagged and moved off site.

03

Interviews

Workers and community members are interviewed away from the site and away from site management, in their own language. Names are never recorded and nothing is attributed to an individual.

04

Evidence check

Every statement in the report is tied to a photograph, document or dated observation. Where accounts conflict, we say so. We never overstate what we find.

If we find child labour

We do not quietly note it and move on. The client is told the same day. The finding goes in the report as a critical issue with the evidence behind it. We do not remove children from a site ourselves, because that can push families into worse situations; we refer the case to the relevant local child protection body and record that referral. The site cannot be reported as compliant until a follow-up visit confirms the issue has been addressed and the cause dealt with. The same applies to forced labour and serious safety failures.
